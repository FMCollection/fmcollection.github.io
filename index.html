Understanding your numbers

What is BMI, and what does it actually tell you?

Body Mass Index compares your weight to your height to give a rough estimate of whether you're in a typical weight range for your height. It doesn't measure body fat directly, and it can be misleading for very muscular people or older adults who've lost muscle mass — but it's still one of the fastest, most widely used starting points doctors and researchers rely on.

What are BMR and TDEE?

Your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) is roughly how many calories your body burns just staying alive — breathing, circulating blood, repairing cells — if you did nothing but rest all day. Your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) adds your activity level on top of that, giving a realistic estimate of how many calories you actually burn in a normal day. This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, which most dietitians consider more accurate than older formulas.

What counts as a healthy "ideal weight"?

Ideal weight formulas — this tool uses the Devine formula — were originally built for medical dosing calculations, not as a personal goal. Treat the result as a reasonable reference range rather than a strict target: frame size, muscle mass, and overall body composition all matter more than a single number on a scale.

What does body fat percentage measure that BMI doesn't?

Body fat percentage estimates how much of your weight is actually fat versus muscle, bone, and water — the distinction BMI can't make. This calculator uses the U.S. Navy method (neck, waist, and for women, hip measurements), which is reasonably accurate for a method that needs no special equipment.

Pace Calculator

Pace is simply time divided by distance, shown as minutes per kilometer. It's the standard way runners compare speed across different distances, so a 5K pace and a marathon pace mean the same thing side by side.

One Rep Max (Epley formula)

1RM = weight × (1 + reps ÷ 30) — estimates the heaviest weight you could lift for a single rep, based on a lighter weight lifted for multiple reps. Useful for programming training percentages without testing a true max, which carries real injury risk.

Target Heart Rate

Estimated maximum heart rate is commonly calculated as 220 minus your age, with training zones expressed as a percentage of that number. It's a widely used starting point, but actual max heart rate varies by 10–20 bpm between people of the same age — treat it as a guide, not an exact figure.

Pregnancy Calculators (Due Date, Conception, Ovulation, Period)

These use Naegele's rule — adding 280 days (40 weeks) to the first day of your last period — the same starting estimate most doctors use. Ovulation and next-period estimates assume a standard 28-day cycle unless you enter your own cycle length. An early ultrasound is significantly more accurate for confirming actual dates.

Macros (Carbs, Protein, Fat)

Once you have a daily calorie target, macros split that number into carbohydrates, protein, and fat based on your goal. Building muscle generally calls for relatively more protein, while cutting often preserves protein while trimming overall calories.

GFR (Glomerular Filtration Rate)

GFR estimates how well your kidneys filter waste from your blood, based on age, gender, and creatinine level. This is a genuine clinical measure — but a concerning number here is not a diagnosis. Real GFR readings come from a lab-verified blood test interpreted by a doctor, not a self-entered estimate.

Body Surface Area (Mosteller formula)

Estimates your total skin surface area from height and weight. It's mainly a clinical tool hospitals use for accurate medication dosing — not something most people need day-to-day, but included here for reference.

BAC (Blood Alcohol Content)

Estimates blood alcohol concentration from drinks consumed, body weight, and gender using the Widmark formula. Individual results vary significantly with metabolism, food intake, and other factors. Never use this estimate to decide whether it's safe to drive or do anything requiring sobriety — if you've been drinking, don't drive.

Frequently asked questions

Is this medical advice?

No. This tool gives general estimates based on standard formulas and is not a substitute for advice from a doctor, dietitian, or other qualified professional — especially if you're pregnant, an athlete, elderly, or managing a medical condition.

Why do my BMI and body fat percentage seem to disagree?

BMI only looks at height and weight, so a muscular person can show a "high" BMI while actually having low body fat. Body fat percentage is generally the more informative of the two for judging health risk.

How often should I recalculate?

Weight and body composition change slowly — recalculating every few weeks is plenty. Day-to-day changes are usually water weight, not fat.

Is my data saved or sent anywhere?

No. Every calculation happens directly in your browser. Nothing you enter is stored, logged, or sent to a server.

Which activity level should I pick for TDEE?

Be honest rather than optimistic — most people overestimate their activity level. If you're unsure, start with "Lightly active" and adjust based on how your weight trends over a few weeks.

Can I use the BAC calculator to decide if I'm okay to drive?

No — never use this or any online calculator to make that decision. Alcohol processing varies too much between individuals to rely on an estimate for something safety-critical. If you've been drinking, don't drive.

What is GFR, and should I be worried about my result?

GFR is a kidney function indicator normally calculated from an actual blood test. A concerning result here is not a diagnosis — talk to a doctor, who will use lab-verified creatinine levels rather than a self-entered estimate.

Are the pregnancy calculators a substitute for an ultrasound?

No. They give the same standard estimate doctors start with based on your last period date, but an early ultrasound is significantly more accurate for confirming actual dates.
